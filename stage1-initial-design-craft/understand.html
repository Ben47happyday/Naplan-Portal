Understand the NAPLAN test

NAPLAN (the National Assessment Program — Literacy and Numeracy) is Australia's annual check-in on how students are tracking in reading, writing, language conventions and numeracy. Every student in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9 sits it. Each section below covers one aspect of the test — open "More details" on any of them for the full picture.

What is NAPLAN?

A national literacy and numeracy check-in, not a pass/fail exam — and it isn't compulsory.

NAPLAN is run by ACARA (the Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority) and sat every year by students in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9 across every state and territory. It's a point-in-time snapshot of how a student is tracking against national literacy and numeracy standards — the results feed into school and system-level planning, not individual report card grades, and no student "passes" or "fails".

Sitting NAPLAN isn't compulsory. Families can request a withdrawal or exemption through their school, but the large majority of students take part each year.

When does it happen?

A national testing window, usually in March — not a single fixed day for the whole country.

Each school schedules its sittings within a national testing window set by ACARA, typically across two to three weeks in March. Your child's school will confirm the exact days closer to the date, and most schools build in catch-up sessions for students who are away or unwell during the main window.

Individual student reports are usually sent home later in the year, well after the testing window closes — schools receive the detailed results first and pass them on.

How is it delivered?

Mostly online, with one paper exception — and it adapts to how a student is going.

From Year 3, Reading, Language Conventions and Numeracy are all completed on a school computer or tablet. Writing stays on paper for Year 3 only, moving fully online from Year 5.

From Year 5 onwards, Reading and Numeracy are adaptive: if a student is answering well, the next questions get a little harder; if they're struggling, the questions ease off. This gives a more precise read on ability than a fixed question set, and means two students in the same room can see different questions.

How results are evaluated

Each domain is reported against four proficiency levels, not a raw score out of 100.

Every domain — Reading, Writing, Language Conventions and Numeracy — sits on the same underlying numeric scale from Year 3 through to Year 9. ACARA sets fixed cut-points on that scale to mark the boundaries between the four proficiency levels, and those cut-points don't move from year to year. That's what lets a school (or a parent) compare a student's Year 5 result with their Year 3 result on the same yardstick, rather than starting fresh each time.

Exceeding

Performing above expectations for their year level.

Strong

Meeting the challenging but reasonable standard expected for their year level.

Developing

Working towards the standard, with some gaps to close.

Needs additional support

Likely to need extra support to build skills for their year level.

The individual report a school sends home shows the proficiency level for each domain, plus roughly where the student sits within that level on a scale bar — so two students both marked "Strong" can still see they landed in different spots. Reports also show how the result compares to the school average and the national average for that year level, but they don't rank individual students against their classmates, and no student-level results are published publicly. Schools receive the full results first, then pass the individual reports on to families, typically in Term 3.

Because the scale is consistent across year levels, the most useful thing to look at usually isn't a single result in isolation but the trend across a student's Year 3, 5, 7 and 9 reports — whether they're tracking with the standard, pulling ahead, or falling behind it over time.
